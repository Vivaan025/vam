1. The pitch in one paragraph

vam is an open-source library and runtime for video-action models: models that look at video and either recognise what action is happening or decide what action an agent should take next. Its claim is simple. On long, real-world video the cost of a model is set by how many frames it looks at, and most frames are wasted. On 200 three-minute THUMOS14 videos, a standard model that picks frames evenly is right 73% of the time with 4 frames. The same model given the right 4 frames is right 89% of the time. That 16-point gap is pure frame selection, and no library closes it today, because choosing frames well costs more than it saves when the decoder is naive. vam builds the missing layer between video files and models: a scheduler that decides which frames deserve compute, and a runtime that makes that decision nearly free by scanning only keyframes, decoding once, and keeping the GPU fed. It already works: with a learned, class-free scorer over keyframes and a coarse-to-fine pass inside each window, vam reaches 0.865 top-1 with 4 frames and 0.925 with 8, matching an oracle that reads the annotations, in about 150 ms per video, where uniform sampling gets 0.720 at the same latency and needs 486 ms to reach 0.880, with the same frozen model. Given only a budget in milliseconds, it picks that configuration itself. PyTorch owns tensors, Hugging Face owns models, vam owns the video-to-action pipeline.

2. Background for a beginner

A video action model takes video in and produces a decision about actions. There are two kinds, and vam is designed to serve both.

  • Action recognition. Input: a clip. Output: a label such as diving or golf swing. This is what the Kinetics, UCF101 and THUMOS14 benchmarks test, and what we measure today.
  • Action prediction. Input: what a robot or agent sees. Output: what it should do next, as a sequence of actions. Models like OpenVLA and the policies in LeRobot do this. Same video pipeline, different head.

Why video is expensive

An image model processes one picture. A video model processes many pictures plus their relationships over time. A transformer's attention cost grows with the square of the number of tokens, and every frame adds hundreds of tokens. A 3-minute video at 30 frames per second has 5,400 frames. No model looks at all of them. Every video model therefore samples: it picks a small number of frames, usually 8 to 32, and ignores the rest.

The frame budget

We call the number of frames a model is allowed to see its budget. A budget of 4 means the model sees four pictures out of thousands and must decide from those. A smaller budget means less compute, less memory and lower latency, which matters on a robot, a phone, or a server handling many streams at once. The question that drives the whole project is: given a budget, which frames should the model see?

Uniform sampling

Uniform sampling is what nearly everyone does: spread the budget evenly across the video. It is free to compute and works well on short clips that have been trimmed to the action. It fails on long video, because the action may occupy a few seconds out of minutes, and evenly spaced frames mostly land on background. We measured exactly this: on THUMOS14, only about 30% of uniformly chosen frames land inside the action, regardless of budget.

Decoding, and why keyframes matter

Decoding turns a compressed video file into pixels. Compressed video stores most frames as differences from earlier frames. Only keyframes (roughly one every few seconds) are stored whole. Reading one arbitrary frame means finding the previous keyframe and decoding forward from it. That is why pulling 16 scattered frames from a 3-minute file costs about half a second on a fast CPU, more than the model forward pass itself. Decoding only keyframes is far cheaper, because there is nothing to decode forward from. That fact is the heart of the runtime we are building.

Frames versus tokens

Inside a transformer each frame becomes a grid of patches, and each patch becomes a token. Dropping a frame drops all of its tokens. A finer lever is to keep a frame but drop its uninformative patches. That is token sparsity, and it is how the budget will eventually control compute inside the model, not just at the decoder. It matters because the model we use, VideoMAE, takes exactly 16 frames: giving it 4 frames today means padding to 16, so a smaller budget saves decode work but not model work. Token sparsity fixes that.

Linear probe

A pretrained model is kept frozen, and one small linear layer is trained on top of its output features. It is the cheapest honest way to measure how good a model's features are on a new dataset. We use it so that every sampler is scored by the same fixed head and only the choice of frames differs. The head was fitted once on UCF101 and then used unchanged on THUMOS14, which shares 20 of its classes.

3. What vam is

vam is a Python library that sits on top of PyTorch and under your model. It owns everything between a video file and a model's input, and everything between a model's output and an action.

The user experience today

import vam

video = vam.load_video("match.mp4")
model = vam.get_model("videomae-base-kinetics")

out = model(video, sampler=vam.UniformSampler(8))
print(out.top_k(3))

for chunk in vam.Stream(0, window=16, hop=8):   # webcam
    print(model(chunk).top_k(1))

Three design rules

  1. Models never touch video. They receive a FrameBatch and return an ActionOutput. The runtime can swap samplers, cache features and stream without any model knowing.
  2. One output type for both meanings of action model. Recognition fills logits; a robot policy fills actions. Everything above the model works for both.
  3. PyTorch is the backend, not the competitor. Custom kernels replace an operator only after profiling shows it is the bottleneck.

Built and tested today: the API types, uniform and motion samplers, the Hugging Face adapter, a linear probe, profiler and feature cache, streaming windows, two dataset loaders (UCF101, THUMOS14), two benchmark harnesses, and 17 tests that pass on a 4 GB laptop GPU and a 32 GB RTX 5090.

Five findings, each of which decides something.

  1. Frame selection matters on long video. Oracle beats uniform by 16 points at 4 frames and 13 at 8. Oracle with 4 frames (0.89) beats uniform with 16 frames (0.885) using a quarter of the frames. On trimmed clips this gap did not exist.
  2. Uniform sampling sees the action only 30% of the time, independent of budget. Blind sampling wastes 70% of the budget on background.
  3. Pixel-motion scoring is worse than uniform at every budget and costs 1.5 s per video. Sports footage is full of camera pans and crowd movement. Frame differencing finds change, not the action. A useful scorer must be semantic.
  4. Decode dominates, by seeking. Random-access decode of 16 frames from a 3-minute file costs about 500 ms, twice the model forward, because each read seeks to a keyframe and decodes forward.
  5. Uniform gets worse at 32 frames because VideoMAE takes exactly 16 and the extra frames are subsampled back at a different stride. Budgets above the model's native length need token-level control, not frame subsampling.

Experiment 6: where the frames land inside a window (2 November 2026)

Three changes to the last stage, each tested alone and together. Ending each window at the next keyframe instead of a fixed 5 seconds is free and gives +2 points at 4 frames (0.815) and +1 at 8 (0.885); it is now the default. Splitting the budget by keyframe score on its own hurts slightly. The big one is a coarse-to-fine pass: decode each chosen window once at thumbnail size, score every third frame with CLIP, and put the frames on the peaks. Combined with the other two it reaches 0.865 at 4 frames and 0.900 at 8, closing most of the remaining gap to the oracle, but it costs 67 to 148 ms more per video, because the CPU decoder charges a flat 35 to 60 ms per seek and 0.3 ms per frame, and refinement must decode whole windows. So it stayed opt-in until experiment 7.

Experiment 7: decode the windows in parallel (2 November 2026)

The chosen windows are independent, so each is now decoded by its own worker: one seek, one short forward read. Decoding no longer grows with the budget (about 90 ms at 4, 8 or 16 frames, where it was 80 to 171 ms serially), and the refinement pass drops from 167 to 326 ms to about 100 ms. Threads turn out to be as good as processes here, because OpenCV releases the interpreter lock while decoding. With refinement every third frame the runtime scores 0.865 / 0.900 / 0.925 at 4 / 8 / 16 frames in 169 / 196 / 195 ms; with every sixth frame and the CLIP scorer in half precision it scores 0.850 / 0.895 / 0.930 in 146 / 152 / 151 ms, which is the new default. Experiment 9: a learned, class-free scorer (2 November 2026)

The zero-shot scorer needs the class names and looks at each keyframe alone. Experiment 9 replaces it with a small head on the same CLIP image embeddings, trained to predict the oracle's window-overlap score from a keyframe and the two keyframes on either side, so it can tell "the action is about to start" from "the action is ending". With only 200 annotated videos, it is trained with five-fold cross-validation by video, so every video is scored by a head that never saw it. On held-out folds it picks a window that overlaps the action 73% of the time against 60% for zero-shot CLIP, and giving it the class names as an extra input changes nothing, so it is class-free. End to end, with refinement, it reaches 0.865 / 0.925 / 0.940 at 4 / 8 / 16 frames in 143 / 153 / 152 ms. At 8 frames that equals the oracle that reads the annotations.

Two honest caveats. The head is trained and tested on the same dataset, so its behaviour on other footage is untested; that is the next item on the roadmap. And one-point differences between head types are inside the noise of 200 videos; the choice of head rests on the scorer-level metrics, which average over thousands of keyframes. The scheduler that turns these curves into a policy for a budget given in milliseconds is experiment 10.

Experiment 10: a scheduler that takes a budget in milliseconds (3 November 2026)

A user does not think in frames. Experiment 10 gives the runtime a budget in milliseconds and nothing else. It fits a small linear cost model on the target machine in six seconds (three videos, four configurations, repeated, with a warm-up pass thrown away), then before each video picks the most accurate configuration whose predicted latency fits, and after the keyframe scan re-solves once with the real keyframe count and window lengths. On the 5090 it chooses 4 frames at 100 ms, 8 frames at 150 and 16 frames with stride-3 refinement from 200 ms up, reaching 0.855 / 0.910 / 0.945 where uniform sampling gets 0.720 at every one of those budgets. On a CPU-only run of the same host the fitted costs are 4 to 50 times larger and the choices differ at every budget: refinement, free on the GPU, is a two-to-three-fold cost there and is only bought when the budget allows it.

Two honest limits. At 150 ms the scheduler is 2.5 points below the best configuration chosen with hindsight, because its predictions are conservative by about 15 ms in a band where configurations differ by only a few ms of true cost; a bigger safety margin makes that worse, not better. And on this shared machine the latency of any configuration, static or scheduled, spreads by 55 to 60 ms between the median and the 90th percentile, so no policy can promise to stay within a 150 or 200 ms budget on every video. The first attempt also taught a lesson: a naive least-squares fit on twelve timings, one of them a stall, produced a cost model with a 282 ms floor that chose 4 frames everywhere up to 150 ms. Median of repeats and a non-negative fit fixed it.

Experiment 11: does any of it hold off the training videos? (3 to 4 November 2026)

Every number so far came from the same 200 validation videos at 320 by 180 pixels. Experiment 11 asks three questions on footage the runtime had not seen. First, the 212 annotated THUMOS14 test videos, which no scorer head was trained on: with refinement the learned scorer reaches 0.868 / 0.901 / 0.915 at 4 / 8 / 16 frames against uniform's 0.769 / 0.816 / 0.858 and the oracle's 0.863 / 0.873 / 0.925. It meets or passes the oracle at 4 and 8 frames. The split has about half the headroom of validation, so the margin over uniform is smaller in points, but the share of the gap closed is higher. Second, the same 50 videos re-encoded to 1080p, to HEVC and to VP9: resolution and codec do not move accuracy, decode cost scales with resolution and the cost model picks it up, but VP9 broke the keyframe scan. Its decoder ignores the request to skip non-keyframes, so the scan returned every frame, five thousand per video, and the scorer spent 34 seconds on each. The fix feeds the decoder only the packets the file marks as keyframes, which brings VP9 to 119 ms and makes H.264 and HEVC scans a quarter faster too. Third, keyframe density: with a keyframe every second the hit rate rises to 0.55; with one every 30 seconds the scan alone falls below uniform, and the coarse-to-fine pass inside each window is what recovers it. Refinement is therefore now on by default. The GPU rerun the next day gave the same accuracies at 149 to 167 ms per video, fixed VP9 at the cost of H.264, and showed the scheduler dropping to 4 frames on 1080p input to hold its budget.

Experiment 12: where it stops working (5 November 2026)

Charades is the opposite kind of video: 30-second indoor clips of someone doing several things, with the action covering 95 percent of every clip. On 1,000 test videos the oracle that samples only inside the action is 2 points worse than even sampling, because they pick nearly the same frames, and so every selector, ours included, ties or loses to uniform: 0.572 for uniform at 16 frames against 0.555 for ours. A scorer retrained on Charades learns to rank its keyframes (rank correlation 0.37 against 0.10 for zero-shot CLIP) and it changes nothing, since every candidate window is already a hit. The runtime itself was unchanged at about 50 ms per clip, and the budget scheduler kept its budget while choosing the wrong configuration, because its accuracy ordering comes from THUMOS14. This is the result the experiment was written to find. Frame selection pays when the action is a small part of the video, as on THUMOS14 where it covers 29 percent, and does nothing when the action is the whole video. Coverage is the variable, the scan can estimate it, and a uniform fallback in the scheduler is the follow-up. What remains is robustness and packaging for a release.

Experiment 13: making it safe to hand to strangers (5 November 2026)

A library fails in the field on inputs a benchmark never sees. Experiment 13 defines four error types, runs the sampler on missing files, empty files, truncated files and a still image renamed as video, and makes every degenerate input (a clip shorter than the budget, a single keyframe, a scorer that returns a constant, windows that would cover most of the clip) answer with plain uniform sampling and say why. Writing the tests found a real bug: the keyframe scan's worker pool forked after thread pools existed and deadlocked two test runs on the server. It now starts workers from a clean helper process and every scan has a timeout. With the uniform fallback the budget scheduler on Charades rises from 0.517 to 0.551, close to uniform's 0.572, while THUMOS14 is unchanged.

Experiment 14: the same runtime, other models (5 November 2026)

Everything so far ran on one model, VideoMAE. Experiment 14 asks whether the runtime cares. First, a second recognition family: torchvision's R(2+1)D-18, with its own crop size and normalisation, plugged in through a 110-line adapter and nothing else changed. The keyframe sampler beats uniform by +9 / +15.5 / +15.5 points at 4 / 8 / 16 frames (0.655 / 0.675 / 0.700 against 0.565 / 0.520 / 0.545) at about 145 ms where uniform costs 135 to 498 ms, and the millisecond scheduler lands within a point of the best static choice at every budget. Second, a policy: OpenVLA-7B, a seven-billion-parameter model that turns one camera frame and an instruction into a 7-D robot arm action. It is an ordinary model to the library, so the streaming interface feeds it frames one at a time at 113 ms a step. On a BridgeData episode it was trained on, its predicted arm motions correlate 0.97 with what the human teleoperator actually did and it opens and closes the gripper where they did; on an indoor Charades clip it flips the gripper 21 times in 60 steps, which is what a model out of its domain should look like. The scheduler's cost model fitted the policy as 11 ms plus 114 ms per frame with zero residual. The work also hardened the library: the policy's own code needs an older transformers release (it runs from a pinned second environment), and the robot videos were AV1, which the default OpenCV build cannot decode, so the stream yielded nothing silently; it now raises an error. Model coverage was the last research item before packaging.

5. The research question

How much of the gap between uniform and oracle frame selection can a runtime close on long video, at a total latency no higher than uniform sampling?

Headroom: 16 points at 4 frames, 13 at 8, 7 at 16 on THUMOS14 validation. Latency baseline: 578 ms per video at 4 frames, of which about 450 ms is decode plus CPU preprocessing. Success means a higher top-1 than uniform at the same budget with total milliseconds at or below uniform's, measured on two GPU tiers.

Three things make this a good question. The headroom is measured, not assumed. The constraint is honest: a scorer that costs more than it saves is not a result, and our own motion sampler already demonstrates that failure mode. And it is a systems question as much as a modelling question, because the latency budget is set by the decoder, not the network.

6. What we are building next

Each piece follows from a specific number above.

Keyframe-scan runtime (built; experiments 3 and 5)

A decoder can be told to decode only keyframes, skipping all inter-frame work. A 3-minute file has roughly 25 to 100 keyframes. Decoding one costs about 1.4 ms in software, and the per-frame Python work holds the interpreter lock, so the scan is split across a pool of worker processes: 46 ms per video on a 24-core host, against 500 ms for one random-access read of 16 frames. The runtime scans keyframes once at low resolution, scores them, picks the best moments, and decodes full-resolution frames only there, reading forward from each keyframe so no seeks are needed.

Semantic scorer (CLIP zero-shot: experiment 3; learned, class-free: experiment 9)

Pixel differencing failed, so the scorer must understand content. The first scorer is CLIP zero-shot similarity between each keyframe and the class names against background prompts, about 8 ms per video on the GPU for all keyframes at once. It beats uniform at every budget with no training. Coarse-to-fine refinement inside each window (experiment 6) lifts it to 0.865 at 4 frames and 0.900 at 8, within 2.5 points of the oracle, and since experiment 7 it is affordable. Experiment 9 replaced the zero-shot scorer with a small head trained to imitate the oracle, which needs no class names and equals the oracle at 8 frames.

Adaptive scheduler (built; experiment 10)

Given a budget in milliseconds rather than frames, decide how many frames to decode, whether to refine and at what stride, from a cost model calibrated on the machine in six seconds. Built and measured in experiment 10: it walks the frontier from 0.855 at 100 ms to 0.945 at 200 ms on its own, and picks different configurations on a CPU-only host. The open items are a per-file decode-cost feature to absorb host jitter, and proving the policy on a second dataset.

GPU preprocessing (built; experiment 4) and GPU decode (next)

Resizing and normalising frames is now done as batched tensor ops on the device, reading each model's exact parameters from its Hugging Face config. The inference span fell from about 300 ms to 20 ms per video with identical predictions. Decoding the chosen windows is seek overhead rather than pixel work: 35 to 60 ms per seek in OpenCV, flat. Since experiment 7 the windows are decoded in parallel, so the stage costs about 90 ms whatever the budget. NVDEC hardware decode (experiment 8) is the only lever left on it.

Token sparsity (from finding 5)

Make compute follow the budget inside the transformer by dropping uninformative patches, so a 4-frame budget costs a quarter of a 16-frame budget in model time, not only in decode time.

Policy adapter (built; experiment 14)

OpenVLA-7B runs through the streaming API as an ordinary model and its actions track the recorded ones on the data it was trained on, so the "both meanings of action" claim is demonstrated, not asserted. What remains before a release is packaging, continuous integration and user documentation.

7. How this is novel, honestly

Adaptive frame sampling is an established research area. Anyone evaluating this project will know that, so the pitch must be precise about what is new.

What already exists

  • Learned frame selectors. SCSampler (Korbar et al., 2019), AdaFrame (Wu et al., 2019), MGSampler, OCSampler (Lin et al., 2022) and FrameExit (Ghodrati et al., 2021) all learn to pick or stop on informative frames. They report accuracy against frame count, and their selectors usually need their own decode pass over many frames, which is the cost our data shows is the real bottleneck.
  • Compressed-domain methods. CoViAR (Wu et al., 2018) and successors read motion vectors and residuals from the compressed stream instead of decoding pixels. Powerful, but tied to one codec's internals and rarely productised.
  • Token reduction. ToMe (Bolya et al., 2023) and related token-merging work reduce transformer cost per frame. Orthogonal to frame choice, and part of our plan.
  • Libraries. PyTorchVideo and MMAction2 give you models and uniform samplers. Decord and NVIDIA DALI give you fast decoders. Hugging Face gives you pretrained weights. None of them makes frame selection a runtime concern with a latency budget, and none ships a scheduler.

What is actually new here

  1. Treating frame selection as a systems problem with a latency budget. Papers report accuracy versus frames. We report accuracy versus wall-clock milliseconds, including the selector's own cost, on real hardware. Our motion-sampler result shows why that matters: a policy can win on frames and lose on time.
  2. Keyframe-scan scheduling. Using the codec's own keyframe structure as the free scan for a semantic scorer, then decoding full frames only at chosen moments. This co-designs the scheduler with the decoder rather than treating decode as a fixed cost.
  3. A reusable runtime, not a paper artefact. The scheduler, cache and decoder sit under any Hugging Face video model and, later, under robot policies, through one interface. The analogy is vLLM for language models: it did not invent attention, it made serving cheap enough that everyone used it.
  4. An honest oracle-based evaluation. Reporting the uniform-to-oracle gap on every benchmark gives a headroom number that most sampling papers leave out, and it makes negative results (like ours on UCF101) informative instead of embarrassing.

What to never claim: that we invented adaptive sampling, that uniform sampling is bad in general, or that the library is "the standard". Claim the measured gap, the latency-constrained framing, and the decoder co-design.

8. Why it is useful

  • Robots and embodied agents. A policy running on an onboard GPU has a fixed millisecond budget per decision. Spending it on the frames that matter is the difference between a usable and an unusable control rate.
  • Edge devices. Phones, cameras and AR glasses cannot decode and process every frame. A runtime that scans keyframes and decodes a handful of frames per second makes video understanding feasible on a 4 GB GPU, which is exactly where we develop.
  • Video agents and long-video question answering. Systems that reason over hour-long video need to find the few relevant minutes before spending model compute. Scheduler plus cache is the missing primitive.
  • Serving at scale. A server handling thousands of streams spends most of its cost on decode and preprocessing, as our profiles show. Halving that is a direct cost reduction independent of the model.
  • Researchers. A common harness that reports accuracy, latency, memory and the oracle gap for any sampler on any Hugging Face model turns sampling papers into comparable results.

9. Pitch for master's applications

Statement-of-purpose paragraph

Video models are bounded by the frames they see, and on real, untrimmed video most frames are background. In my open-source project vam I built the infrastructure to measure and attack this. Using a frozen VideoMAE backbone with a fixed linear head, I showed that on 200 untrimmed THUMOS14 videos, oracle frame selection beats uniform sampling by 16 points of top-1 accuracy at a 4-frame budget, while on trimmed UCF101 clips the gap vanishes. Profiling on an RTX 5090 showed the model forward is a minority of wall time; decode seeking and CPU preprocessing dominate, and a naive motion-based selector loses on latency even when it matches on accuracy. I am now building a decode-aware runtime that scans only codec keyframes for a cheap semantic scorer and decodes full frames only where they matter, evaluated against a latency budget rather than a frame count. The project combines GPU systems work (asynchronous decode, memory management, kernel-level profiling) with a precise empirical question, and it is the kind of systems-for-ML research I want to pursue in graduate study.

Talking points

  • One precise question with measured headroom: 16 points at 4 frames.
  • A negative result you understand: motion sampling fails on sports footage because it detects change, not actions, and it costs 1.5 s. Explaining why is more impressive than hiding it.
  • Systems depth: you can explain keyframe seeking, why random access costs 500 ms, why the GPU sits idle, and what NVDEC changes.
  • Two GPU tiers: a 4 GB laptop and a 32 GB RTX 5090, with the same code and the same JSONL output format.
  • Engineering hygiene: tests on both machines, reproducible benchmarks, results committed with raw CSVs.

Questions you will be asked, and answers

"Isn't adaptive sampling already solved?"
Studied, not solved, and almost never evaluated against latency. Our data shows a selector can win on frame count and lose on time. The contribution is the runtime that makes selection cheaper than the frames it saves.
"Why not just use a bigger model or more frames?"
Because the cost is in decode and preprocessing, not the model. Uniform at 32 frames was slower and less accurate than uniform at 16. More frames is not the lever.
"Is the oracle a fair comparison?"
It is not a method, it is a ceiling. Reporting it is what makes the headroom honest. Any real scheduler is judged on how much of that ceiling it reaches.
"What is the single result you are proudest of?"
Oracle with 4 frames beats uniform with 16 frames. A quarter of the frames, higher accuracy, if you know where to look. Everything else is about learning where to look cheaply.
"What would you do with more compute?"
Train the scorer to imitate the oracle on THUMOS14 training annotations, evaluate on ActivityNet, and add a robot policy adapter to show the runtime transfers to action prediction.

10. Glossary

Action recognition
Classifying which action a video shows.
Action prediction
Deciding what an agent should do next from what it sees.
Adapter
A thin wrapper that lets an existing model implement vam's interface without being rewritten.
Budget
The number of frames, or the milliseconds, a model is allowed to spend on one video.
CLIP
A model that maps images and text into the same space, so an image can be scored against a phrase like "a person diving" without training.
Codec
The compression scheme of a video file, for example H.264.
Decode
Turning compressed video into pixel frames.
Feature cache
Storing a model's intermediate outputs for frames so they are not recomputed when nothing changed.
Hit rate
The share of selected frames that fall inside an annotated action segment.
Keyframe (I-frame)
A frame stored in full in the compressed stream; every other frame is a difference from a neighbour.
Linear probe
A single linear layer trained on frozen features; a standard way to measure feature quality.
NVDEC
NVIDIA's hardware video decoder on the GPU.
Oracle sampler
A sampler that uses ground-truth annotations; the upper bound for frame selection.
Random access
Reading an arbitrary frame, which forces a seek to the previous keyframe and decoding forward.
Sampler
The component that turns a video and a budget into a list of frame indices.
Scheduler
A sampler that also reasons about time and compute cost, deciding how many frames and where under a latency budget.
THUMOS14
A dataset of untrimmed sports videos with the start and end of each action annotated.
Token sparsity
Dropping uninformative patches inside a transformer so compute falls without dropping whole frames.
Top-1
The fraction of videos where the model's highest-scoring class is correct.
UCF101
A dataset of 13,320 short trimmed clips in 101 action classes.
Uniform sampling
Picking frames at equal spacing across the video.
VideoMAE
A video transformer pretrained by reconstructing masked patches; takes 16 frames.

11. Reading list, in order

  1. VideoMAE (Tong et al., 2022). The backbone we use. Read to understand why it needs 16 frames and what its tokens are.
  2. TSN: Temporal Segment Networks (Wang et al., 2016). The origin of sparse uniform sampling for video. Explains why uniform works on trimmed clips.
  3. SCSampler (Korbar et al., 2019). A cheap clip-level saliency sampler. The closest prior idea to a semantic scorer; note what it costs to run.
  4. AdaFrame (Wu et al., 2019) and FrameExit (Ghodrati et al., 2021). Learned, adaptive frame selection and early exit. Read for the evaluation protocols and for what they do not report: wall-clock latency.
  5. OCSampler (Lin et al., 2022). One-pass frame selection; compare its selector cost with our keyframe-scan idea.
  6. CoViAR: Compressed Video Action Recognition (Wu et al., 2018). Using the compressed stream directly. The strongest precedent for decoder co-design.
  7. Token Merging (ToMe) (Bolya et al., 2023). The token-sparsity lever we plan to add.
  8. vLLM: Efficient Memory Management for LLM Serving with PagedAttention (Kwon et al., 2023). Not about video. Read it as the model of what a runtime contribution looks like and how it is evaluated.
  9. THUMOS Challenge 2014 dataset page and the UCF101 paper (Soomro et al., 2012). Know your benchmarks.
  10. OpenVLA (Kim et al., 2024) and the LeRobot documentation. The action-prediction side of the library, for the policy adapter.

Reading advice: for each paper write three lines in your own words: what they measured, what they left out, and what vam would change. That list becomes your related-work section and your interview answers.
